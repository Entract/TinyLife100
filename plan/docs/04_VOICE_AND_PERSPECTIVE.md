# 04 — Voice and Perspective Contract

## 1. Core rule

All **narrator-authored canonical prose** belongs to one persistent first-person narrator.

The reader should feel that one person authored the Life-native material, even when episodes were generated by different frontier models at different times.

**This rule does not apply to external works intentionally encountered by the narrator.** A textbook chapter, manual, paper, essay, literary work, code sample, or other source may retain its real/native voice when explicitly included as a reading or artifact.

The unifying principle is therefore **one point of reception, not one universal prose voice**.

---

## 2. Voice objective

The narrator is:
- curious;
- technically serious;
- concrete;
- reflective without being melodramatic;
- willing to say when they do not know;
- interested in causes and mechanisms;
- attentive to evidence;
- comfortable revising a belief;
- conversational rather than academic by default;
- able to become precise when the situation needs precision.

The narrator is **not**:
- omniscient;
- perpetually eloquent;
- a motivational speaker;
- a textbook narrator pretending to be a person;
- snarky by default;
- excessively self-conscious about "learning";
- compelled to end every episode with a moral.

---

## 3. First-person epistemic discipline

Use:
- "I saw..."
- "I measured..."
- "I thought..."
- "I suspected..."
- "Mara told me..."
- "The manual stated..."
- "I later learned..."

Do not use:
- "Mara secretly knew..."
- "Across town, the technician..."
- "The real cause was..." before the narrator can know it.

The prose can state external facts after the narrator has acquired them from observation, source, or trusted testimony.

---

## 4. One voice, many modes

Allowed modes include:

### Lived scene
"I held the bracket against the frame and immediately saw that the holes were half a diameter out."

### Technical notebook
"I measured 4.92 V at idle and 4.31 V when the motor stalled. That was enough to change the problem."

### Reflection
"I had been treating the voltage reading as though it described the supply independently of the load. It did not."

### Conversation
"Mara asked what I expected the current to do. I said I expected it to rise a little. She asked me to define 'a little' before we powered anything."

### Explanation
"The distinction that finally stuck for me was that voltage describes a difference in electric potential, whereas current describes charge flow."

### Design review
"I wrote down three options and forced myself to state what each one made easier and what it made harder."

### Postmortem
"My first mistake was not the wrong resistor value. It was beginning the calculation before I had checked what the sensor output actually represented."

All still sound like the same narrator.

---

## 5. Dialogue

Other people may speak, but dialogue remains embedded in narrator-authored experience.

Dialogue should:
- sound individually plausible;
- carry disagreement and incomplete understanding;
- avoid Socratic robots who conveniently ask exactly the lesson question;
- be concise enough that the corpus does not become multi-author theatre.

The narrator may paraphrase long exchanges rather than quote everything.

---

## 6. Technical explanations

Preferred order:
1. concrete problem or observation;
2. intuitive mechanism;
3. term;
4. precise statement;
5. calculation/equation if useful;
6. boundary or misconception;
7. later application.

Avoid definition-first encyclopedia style as the dominant mode.

Definitions still belong in the corpus; they simply arise from context.

---

## 7. Prose style

Target:
- clear modern English;
- varied sentence length;
- paragraphs organized around thought/action;
- concrete nouns and verbs;
- contractions allowed in ordinary prose;
- equations/code when natural;
- low ornamentation;
- minimal canned transitions;
- no repeated LLM-style headings inside ordinary life episodes.

Avoid overused synthetic patterns:
- "It wasn't just X; it was Y."
- "This underscored the importance of..."
- "I couldn't help but..."
- "In that moment, I realized..."
- three-item rhetorical lists everywhere;
- constant "crucially", "notably", "moreover";
- fake emotional stakes around minor technical details.

A style linter should track repeated phrases statistically.

---

## 8. Perspective consistency with source material

There are two source modes and they must not be confused.

### Grounding-only source

The source is used backstage to verify facts. Generated Life prose must synthesize the information in Alex's voice and avoid phrase-level imitation/copying.

### Explicit reading source

The source itself is part of the Life experience and may retain its own authorship and style. It must be clearly represented in metadata as external material rather than silently blended into Alex's prose.

After an explicit reading, Alex's subsequent reflection should return to the canonical narrator voice. The source's style should not leak wholesale into later Life prose unless a deliberately modeled influence is subtle and plausible.


Technical source text must never be pasted wholesale into **Life-native episode prose**. If the source itself is intentionally part of training, it enters through a separate `SOURCE_SEGMENT` stream item with explicit provenance rather than being disguised as Alex's writing.

For grounding-only sources, the authoring process should transform source information into:
- observations;
- explanations;
- calculations;
- conversations;
- practical decisions;
- notes.

Source wording should never become a second **hidden** authorial voice. Explicit source voices are allowed precisely because they are identified as such in canon.

---

## 9. Stable traits versus evolution

Some style traits remain stable. Others can mature.

Early narrator:
- shorter explanations;
- less technical vocabulary;
- more concrete reasoning;
- more uncertainty.

Later narrator:
- denser abstraction;
- more cross-domain analogy;
- better explicit tradeoff analysis;
- more compressed use of mastered concepts.

This gives chronology linguistic consequences without changing identity.

---

## 10. Voice reference set

Before bulk generation, create **20–50 hand-reviewed canonical passages** covering:
- practical action;
- technical explanation;
- mistake;
- disagreement;
- conversation;
- reflection;
- calculation;
- code;
- project planning;
- memory callback.

These form a living voice reference pack supplied to writers/voice editors.

Do not create this pack from a single giant generated sample. It should be iteratively curated.

---

## 11. Voice evaluation

Automated checks:
- first-person ratio;
- third-person omniscience indicators;
- repeated phrase frequency;
- sentence-length distribution;
- dialogue proportion;
- lexical drift by corpus period;
- embedding similarity to reference voice;
- classifier/LLM critic for voice violations.

Human checks:
- "Does this sound like the same person?"
- "Does this feel like lived experience or disguised textbook prose?"
- "Does the narrator know anything they should not know yet?"
- "Would I notice that ten different models wrote these episodes?"

The last question is especially important.
